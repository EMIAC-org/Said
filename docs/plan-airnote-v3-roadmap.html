1 What problem are we solving?

Speech-to-text (STT) services like Deepgram are great at common English. But they fail predictably on proper nouns, technical terms, brand names, and anything domain-specific. When you say "Kubernetes", Deepgram might output "cubinatis" or "cooper natives". When you say "GitHub", it might write "get hub".

Watch what happens with the word "Kubernetes":

You speak
"Deploy Kubernetes to production"
Deepgram hears
"Deploy cubinatis to production"
AirNote corrects
"Deploy Kubernetes to production"
LLM polishes
"Deploy Kubernetes to production."
Think of it like this
AirNote sits between Deepgram and your screen as a spell-checker tuned to your vocabulary. Like how Microsoft Word remembers technical words you add to its dictionary, AirNote remembers the words YOU say. The difference: AirNote learns by watching you fix mistakes, not by you adding words manually.

2 How AirNote learns — the simple version

Three actors interact every time you dictate. Understanding their roles is the foundation for everything else in this document.

ACTOR 1
Deepgram (the ear)
Cloud STT service. Converts your audio to text. Great at common English, weak at brand names and jargon. Cannot be trained on your voice — it's a black box.
audio → "I love using git hub"
ACTOR 2
AirNote (the brain)
Runs locally on your Mac. Watches what Deepgram outputs, what the LLM polishes, and what you keep. Learns from every edit you make. Stores your vocabulary in a local SQLite database.
Deepgram output → AirNote fixes → your screen
ACTOR 3
Groq LLM (the writer)
Polishes the corrected transcript. Fixes filler words, stutters, punctuation. Sees your vocabulary as hints. Lives in the cloud but treats every recording as standalone — no memory.
"i i i need github" → "I need GitHub."
YOUR ROLE
The teacher
When AirNote gets something wrong, you fix it. Every fix becomes training data. After 1-3 fixes (depending on word type), AirNote learns the pattern permanently and applies it automatically going forward.
You type "Kubernetes" → AirNote remembers forever

The learning loop, step by step:

Step 1 · First time
You say "GitHub" → typed as "get hub" → you fix it to "GitHub"
Step 2 · AirNote records
Stores alias: "get hub" → "GitHub" in local database
Step 3 · Neural training
Generates 30 likely variations ("git hub", "githab", "get-hub"...) and trains a tiny model on them in 3 seconds
Step 4 · Next time
You say "GitHub" → Deepgram outputs "git hub" → AirNote auto-corrects → "GitHub" on screen ✓

3 The four correction layers

AirNote doesn't run one big algorithm — it runs four specialized ones in sequence. Each catches a different kind of mistake. The neural model (Layer 4) is the newest addition; it shipped this week.

Layer 1
Exact Memory
"Have I seen this exact misheard word before?" Lookup table.
"cubinatis" → "Kubernetes"
Layer 2
Context Rules
"The word 'corps' near 'ka' or 'ki' means the company name." Bigram patterns.
"corps ka launch" → "Datadog ka launch"
Layer 3
Fuzzy Match
"This token looks similar to something I know." Edit-distance check.
"docker file" stays "docker file" — too different
Layer 4
Neural Model
"Even though I've never seen this exact word, my model thinks it's close to a known term." MLP scoring.
"cooper natives" → "Kubernetes" (new variant)
Real-world analogy
Imagine a librarian with four tools: a card index (Layer 1: exact lookup), a topic catalogue (Layer 2: context patterns), a fuzzy spelling checker (Layer 3: similar words), and a brain (Layer 4: judgment about new books). Each tool kicks in only if the previous ones didn't find an answer. The brain is the most powerful but also the most cautious — multiple safety nets prevent it from being wrong.

Why four layers, not one?

Layer When it wins When it fails Why we keep it
Exact Same garble repeats Deepgram invents a new garble Fastest, zero false-positive risk
Context Real English word is meant as brand ("corps" → company) No surrounding context clues Distinguishes "Spring framework" from "spring season"
Fuzzy Small typo of an active rule's target Token is very different from anything known Conservative — only fires on high-confidence matches
Neural Never-seen-before garble of a known term Common English/Hindi word that sounds vaguely like vocab Generalization. Catches what the other 3 can't.

4 The big v3 feature: shared vocabulary buckets

Right now every user has one vocabulary list, private to them. If five teammates all say "Kubernetes", each one has to teach AirNote independently. This is the biggest UX cost we have.

v3 introduces two vocabulary buckets: a private one (your own learnings) and a shared one (your team's collective knowledge). When one teammate corrects a brand name, everyone benefits.

Rahul joins the team on Monday. He installs AirNote at 10am. By 10:05am, his copy of AirNote has already pulled "Kubernetes", "Kafka", "GitHub", and 50 other team terms from the shared bucket. When he says any of them, AirNote gets it right on the first try. Zero teaching required.

How the lookup priority works:

Priority Source Why
1st Personal bucket (your aliases, your edits) You know your own vocabulary best. Your "Anish" wins over team's "Anish".
2nd Shared bucket (company terms) Team consensus. Used when personal bucket doesn't have a match.
3rd Global / built-in (English dictionary) Fallback. Catches "the", "and", etc.

The neural model sees both buckets

When AirNote retrains the neural model after a user edit, it pulls from personal + shared as the candidate pool. The model learns to score against both. The shared bucket dramatically improves cold-start quality — a new user gets ~50 high-quality candidates instead of starting from zero.

How auto-promotion to shared bucket works

A term graduates from "personal" to "shared" when 3+ teammates independently correct the same word. If Abhishek, Rahul, and Anish all teach AirNote that "emiac" means "EMIAC", the term enters the shared bucket automatically. No admin involvement needed for organic team vocabulary.

An admin can also seed the shared bucket directly (CSV import or web admin panel) for known terms — useful at onboarding time.

5 The 10 gaps to close in v3

Ranked by user-impact. Each has a concrete fix with a measurable success criterion.

GAP 1 · CRITICAL
No shared vocabulary across team
Every user teaches their AirNote from scratch. Painful at company scale.
5 engineers × 50 brand terms × 3 corrections each = 750 wasted edits
Fix: Two-bucket architecture (personal + shared) with auto-promotion at 3+ teammate confirmations. See §4.
GAP 2 · CRITICAL
Deepgram drops words entirely
When Deepgram outputs nothing for a word, no correction layer can recover it. User has to manually star the term to get keyterm biasing.
User says "Anthropic" → Deepgram outputs "" → AirNote can't help
Fix: Auto-star after 3 consecutive add-backs. Tells Deepgram "listen for this word" automatically.
GAP 3 · HIGH
Cold-start quality with < 10 vocab terms
Fresh users see degraded corrections for 1-2 weeks because the neural model has too few positives vs negatives.
3 vocab terms → 60 positives, 6000 negatives → model too conservative
Fix: Cap dictionary negatives at 10× positives. Plus shared bucket from §4 gives ~50 candidates day 1.
GAP 4 · HIGH
No regression tests for the correction engine
Every model change is a flying-blind deployment. We have no way to detect that a tweak made things worse.
Last week: tightened the safety net, broke 3 valid corrections silently
Fix: 100-case golden dataset in CI. Block PRs that regress accuracy > 2%.
GAP 5 · HIGH
No production quality telemetry
We track vocabulary count but not correction quality. False positive rate? Unknown. False negative rate? Unknown.
User reverts 5% of corrections → we never find out
Fix: Emit correction decisions + user outcomes to an analytics endpoint (opt-in). Compute weekly P/R/F1.
GAP 6 · MEDIUM
Multi-token corrections (phrases)
Tier 2 only handles single-word terms. "New York" or "San Francisco" never become vocabulary entries.
"san francisco" stays "san francisco" — but should become "San Francisco"
Fix: Bigram scorer. Either separate ONNX model for 2-token candidates or token-merge before scoring.
GAP 7 · MEDIUM
Only English / Hindi / Hinglish supported
No Spanish, French, Portuguese, Japanese, Chinese support. International expansion blocked.
Spanish user says "buenos días" → no phonetic rules to help correct it
Fix: Pluggable language module. Ship Spanish + French in v3 as proof of architecture.
GAP 8 · MEDIUM
ASR confusions are English-centric
Our augmentation pairs (b↔p, d↔t) are English-grounded. Misses Hindi retroflex confusions (ड↔ठ).
"वडापाव" misheard as "वठापाव" — we don't generate this variant in training
Fix: Mine ASR confusion matrix from real user data instead of hardcoding.
GAP 9 · LOW
Per-context vocabulary
Coding terms (kubectl, npm) and meeting terms (client names) share one global vocab. Cross-pollution at scale.
"react" the verb gets corrected to "React" the framework in a casual chat
Fix: Optional context labels per recording. Filter active vocab by tag.
GAP 10 · LOW
Retrain time at scale
5s with 12 vocab terms. Projected 20s at 200 terms. The micro-retrain re-builds the entire training set instead of patching it.
200 vocab × 5 ASR confusions × 30 variants = ~30K examples per retrain
Fix: Example cache keyed by term hash. Only invalidate the changed term.

6 The 6-week execution plan

Three phases. Each phase ships independently. Phase 1 unblocks Phase 2 unblocks Phase 3.

Weeks 1–2 · Foundation
Buckets & Telemetry

The unblock. Two-bucket architecture + measurement infrastructure.

  • Personal + Shared bucket schema: add org_id and scope columns to vocabulary table. Migration is backward-compatible.
  • Shared sync API: control-plane endpoints to push/pull team vocabulary. Reuses existing auth.
  • Auto-promotion rule: term enters shared bucket after 3+ teammate confirmations.
  • Golden test dataset: 100 real STT-error pairs in CI.
  • Decision-event telemetry: emit corrections + outcomes to analytics (opt-in).
Weeks 3–4 · Coverage
Edge Cases & Quality

Close the 5 high-impact gaps. Measurable accuracy lift.

  • Auto-star on drop: after 3 add-backs of a known term, star it for Deepgram biasing.
  • Adaptive negative sampling: cap dictionary negatives at 10× positives. Fixes cold-start.
  • Real ASR confusion mining: derive augmentation matrix from actual user aliases, not hardcoded rules.
  • Bigram scorer: separate ONNX model for "New York"-style 2-token corrections.
  • Wider Hindi commons list: ~200 more regional Hinglish words.
Weeks 5–6 · Scale
International & Performance

Make AirNote work outside Hinglish. Sub-second retraining.

  • Pluggable language module: per-language romanizer + phonetic key + common-words. Ship Spanish + French.
  • Per-context vocabulary tags: "code", "meeting", "personal". Filter active vocab by context.
  • Incremental retraining: example cache keyed by term hash. Sub-second retrain at any vocab size.
  • Model staleness watchdog: nightly drift detection, triggers retrain on degradation.
  • Public quality dashboard: internal page showing weekly correction accuracy per cohort.

7 Day-one insane quality

Right now AirNote gets better over time. The first week is rough — the user has to teach it dozens of corrections before it really shines. That's our biggest UX problem. By v3, day-one should already feel magical.

The current problem
A new user installs AirNote, dictates "Kubernetes" five times on day one. AirNote misspells it five times. They give up before the model has enough data to learn. The first impression decides whether they stay or churn.

Four levers to make day-one excellent

LEVER 1
Ship a pre-trained baseline model
Bundle a tiny ONNX model trained on 500+ common tech, brand, and proper-noun terms (Kubernetes, GitHub, ChatGPT, Anthropic, Vercel, FastAPI, etc.). The app already corrects these from minute one, even with zero personal data.
First time you say "Kubernetes" → it just works. No teaching needed.
LEVER 2
Industry-specific starter buckets
At install time, the user picks their industry (Tech, Finance, Health, Legal, Design). We pre-seed their shared bucket with ~50 high-frequency terms for that industry. Their AirNote knows the vocabulary of their domain before they speak a word.
Tech user gets: Docker, Kubernetes, Terraform, Grafana, Anthropic... Finance user gets: IRR, EBITDA, IPO, NPV, AUM...
LEVER 3
5-question onboarding flow
Five quick questions at first launch: your name, your team's name, your top 3 product names, brands you mention daily, languages you mix. Each answer pre-populates the personal bucket. Takes 30 seconds. Saves weeks of teaching.
Name: Abhishek → "Abhi", "abhishek", "abishek" auto-aliased.
LEVER 4
Synthetic warm-up training
After onboarding, generate ~200 synthetic ASR distortions per term using our existing variant generator and train the neural model in the background before the user's first recording. By the time they speak, the model already knows their vocabulary inside out.

The day-one experience, before and after

v2.2 (today)
Day 1: 50% accuracy on brands. User corrects 30 times. Day 7: 90% accuracy.
v3 (target)
Day 1: 90% accuracy on brands. User corrects 3 times. Day 7: 99% accuracy.

8 The edge case mountain

AirNote's correction engine works beautifully on the happy path. But there's a long tail of edge cases that cost user trust when they fail. We've mapped ~30 categories of edge cases. Here are the ones that need attention in v3, grouped by impact.

High-impact edge cases (must fix in v3)

EDGE 1
Numbers in mixed contexts
"Twelve thousand five hundred" → "12,500" works. But "twelve thousand IPO at fifty crore" should preserve "IPO" while converting the numbers. Today this is brittle.
"dus baje meeting hai" → "10 baje meeting hai" ✓
"twenty five percent of 12 lakh" → currently mangles
EDGE 2
Structured tokens (emails, URLs, paths)
"anish at the rate gmail dot com" should become "anish@gmail.com". Works in tray-polish mode, weaker in voice mode. URLs and file paths have similar gaps.
"check localhost colon three thousand slash api" → "check localhost:3000/api"
EDGE 3
Code identifiers in casual speech
"Set the snake case variable max underscore retries to ten" — should preserve "max_retries" as a code token. Currently inconsistent.
"camelCase the function name as getUserData" → output should keep "getUserData"
EDGE 4
Heavy code-switching mid-sentence
"Yaar this Kubernetes pod ka memory limit zyada hai kya?" — three language switches in one sentence. Currently the LLM sometimes flattens to one language.
Need to preserve the natural mix exactly as spoken
EDGE 5
Background noise / partial words
When Deepgram is uncertain, it emits low-confidence tokens. We sometimes confidently "correct" these into vocab terms when really the audio was unclear.
User mumbled something near "Kafka" → we emit "Kafka" when we shouldn't have
EDGE 6
Same-vocab homophones
User has both "Anish" (name) and "Anit" (different person) in vocab. Deepgram outputs something in between. Which one do we pick? Currently it's basically a coin flip.
Need user-specific phonetic distinguishers + context

Medium-impact edge cases (best-effort fix)

Category Example Plan
Currencies + units "₹5 lakh" / "$500K" / "fifty cents" Unit-aware token recognizer in polish prompt
Time + date formats "day after tomorrow" / "next Friday" / "saat baje" Date-NER + locale-aware formatting
Acronyms vs words "API" vs "happy" / "SDK" vs "stuck" Use term_type=acronym + casing memory
Punctuation by speech "comma", "question mark", "new line" Spoken-punctuation mode toggle in settings
Quotes / parens "quote unquote", "in brackets" Pattern recognition in polish prompt
Stuttering speech "I I I want want to" Already handled in v2.2 polish prompt ✓
Mid-sentence restarts "I was going to the, I went to the store" Already handled in v2.2 polish prompt ✓
Regional Hindi variants Punjabi-tinted, Marathi-tinted, Bengali-tinted Expand Hindi commons list per region
Voice quality (whisper/loud) Speech with breath / against wind STT-level — needs Deepgram tuning or local model
Long pauses → run-on "...and then. I went..." Sentence-boundary smoothing in polish

Why we can fix these systematically (not case-by-case)

Each edge case looks unique, but most fit into 4 buckets: token-classification (number? code? brand? acronym?), context-awareness (which homophone in this sentence?), confidence-thresholding (was Deepgram unsure?), and format-normalization (URLs/emails/paths). Once we build the token classifier in v3, dozens of these collapse into one fix.

9 Training time at 200+ vocab terms

Today's training pipeline works great at 12 vocab terms (5 seconds). At 200 vocab terms it projects to ~30 seconds — which is too slow because we retrain after every user edit. We need this to stay sub-2-seconds at any vocab size.

Why training time grows

Vocab size Positive examples Negative examples Total examples Train time
12 terms (today) ~3,000 ~18,000 ~21,000 5 sec
50 terms ~12,000 ~80,000 ~92,000 ~12 sec
100 terms ~25,000 ~170,000 ~195,000 ~22 sec
200 terms ~50,000 ~340,000 ~390,000 ~35 sec

Four optimizations to keep training sub-2-seconds

OPT 1
Per-term example cache
Cache the generated ASR variants for each term, keyed by term hash. When the user corrects only one term, only invalidate that term's variants. The other 199 terms keep their cached examples.
200 terms with 1 changed → regenerate variants for 1 term, reuse 199 from cache
OPT 2
Adaptive variant count
At 12 terms we generate 30 variants per term. At 200 terms we don't need that. Scale variants per term inversely with vocab size: at 200 terms, 10 variants is enough.
v2.2: fixed 30 variants × 200 = 6000 variants
v3: 10 variants × 200 = 2000 variants (still plenty)
OPT 3
Negative example reservoir
Hard negatives from /usr/share/dict/words don't depend on the user's vocabulary. Build them ONCE per app version, ship them as a static asset. Skip dictionary mining entirely at retrain time.
Saves ~3-5 seconds of dictionary scanning on every retrain
OPT 4
Background incremental retraining
After a single edit, run a 1-second "micro retrain" that updates the model with just the new term's examples (warm start from previous weights). Once a week, run a full "deep retrain" overnight that regenerates everything from scratch.
Micro: 1s after every edit. Deep: 30s once a week, when user isn't actively using.

Why this matters
Retraining isn't a one-time operation — it fires every time the user makes a correction. At 200 vocab, a 30-second training freeze after each edit would be unusable. With the optimizations above, the user never notices retraining is happening. The neural model becomes invisible infrastructure.
